# Chingis’ writing memory

Updated: 30 October 2026. Applies to blog ideas, drafts, revisions, translations and publication copy.

Chingis’ feedback on “Faster Than the Forecast”: the article reads slowly, feels slightly AI-written, and needs substantial cuts. Future writing should be quicker, more personal and easier to follow, including for people outside IT.

## Voice

The requested influences are Linus Tech Tips, ThePrimeagen and Theo. Use broad qualities: accessible explanations, practical demonstrations, blunt engineering judgment, curiosity and scepticism about hype. Write in Chingis’ own voice; borrow neither catchphrases nor a creator’s mannerisms.

Sound like a knowledgeable person explaining something interesting over coffee. Have an opinion and show what supports it. Dry humour can expose an absurd situation; it earns space when it helps the point. There is no joke quota, manufactured rant or need to sound constantly outraged.

## Drafting sequence

1. **Choose one point.** Write a private sentence answering: “What should the reader understand or do differently?” For a nontechnical audience, name the consequence: access to an account, lost time, money, private information or control over a decision. The outline is ready when every section advances that point.
2. **Open with the interesting part.** Start with a concrete observation, consequence or disagreement. Reach the article’s main claim within the first two short paragraphs. Introduce background when the reader needs it. A broad statement about how quickly technology changes is usually expendable.
3. **Explain through the work.** Follow a real example from problem to result. Describe jargon by what it does or changes for the reader; retain technical detail that explains the mechanism. Mark hypothetical examples clearly. Personal experiences, failures and results must come from Chingis’ material, never invention.
4. **Cut whole passages.** Give each paragraph one job: claim, example, evidence or consequence. Delete a paragraph if the surrounding text already does that job. Compress repeated setup, summaries and qualifications before polishing individual sentences. For a slow existing draft, try removing a third, then check what genuinely needs restoring.
5. **Read aloud and verify.** Mix short emphatic sentences with natural longer explanations. Join fragments that sound like a sequence of social-media slogans. Check that every retained number and factual claim still has support after cutting. Finish when the opening makes the stakes clear, each section adds something, and the ending gives one useful takeaway without retelling the article.

## Length and structure

Working defaults: 800–1,200 words for a focused article; 300–600 for a short update, excluding references. These are starting ranges, not quotas. A longer investigation earns its length through additional evidence or explanation. Use headings where the subject changes, rather than forcing every section into the same shape. End once the point lands.

Keep evidence close to the claim. State an essential limitation once, where misreading would matter. Put secondary methodology in source notes. Cutting caveats must not turn a survey into a causal finding, a benchmark into a promise, or suspicion about a hack into confirmed attribution. Separate confirmed incident details, inference and open questions.

## Illustrative cuts

These examples demonstrate editing, not reported events:

- “It is important to understand that a successful demonstration does not necessarily establish operational reliability.” → “A working demo tells you little about the next outage.”
- “This raises an important question about responsibility.” → Ask the actual question: “Who fixes it when it breaks?”
- “In today’s rapidly evolving technological landscape, users face increasingly complex choices.” → Name the choice and its consequence, or remove the sentence.

## Translation and publication

Edit the argument before translating. Give German its own natural rhythm, real umlauts and gender stars; preserve evidence, dates and meaning rather than English sentence shapes. Check both versions for repeated explanations.

Titles and descriptions should tell readers what they will get. Search keywords belong where they clarify the subject. Carry forward existing publication instructions; saving a future topic does not itself authorize publishing it.
