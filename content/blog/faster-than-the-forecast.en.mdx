<aside className="article-context">Written in September 2026 from my work at beWirken. First published on October 30, 2026. The figures below describe the periods stated in the sources.</aside>

In February 2025 I started as the developer at beWirken, a small non-profit in Germany that works on school development and democracy education. The same month, Anthropic released the first research preview of Claude Code. I did not notice the coincidence at the time. I do now.

My job description covers development for client projects, maintenance of the organization's IT infrastructure, advice on IT security, and the AI strategy. In practice that means a WordPress shop, a Moodle server, a Django application, a couple of Next.js tools, a HubSpot integration, and the servers under all of it. On paper it is one title. In practice it is five jobs. I build. I keep things alive. I check what can be trusted. I help decide what should exist. I run what is live.

Nineteen months ago I typed most of my code. Today I read most of it. An agent drafts; I specify, review, test, and decide whether it ships. The change has been faster than I expected. But the most useful question is what happens to the rest of the job when writing the code becomes easier.

<h2 id="how-fast-is-fast">How fast is fast</h2>

One way to measure the pace comes from METR, a research group that compares AI performance with the time a skilled human needs for a task. In March 2025 it reported that the length of tasks frontier models could complete at a 50 percent success rate had doubled roughly every seven months since 2019, reaching about one hour.<sup id="cite-1-1"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a></sup> Its January 2026 update estimated a shorter doubling time of 89 days when looking only at models released since 2024.<sup id="cite-2-1"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Source 2">[2]</a></sup> Its May report put the February–March 2026 public frontier at around twelve hours on this measure, with a very wide estimated interval of five to sixty-one hours.<sup id="cite-3-1"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Source 3">[3]</a></sup>

Those figures suggest rapid progress, but they need careful reading. The 89-day estimate uses a shorter period and an updated task suite; METR's longer historical fit still gives roughly seven months. A 50 percent time horizon estimates the human task duration at which a model succeeds half the time; it does not measure twelve hours of reliable autonomous work. The estimates are uncertain, especially near the limits of the test.<sup id="cite-2-2"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Source 2">[2]</a></sup><sup id="cite-4-1"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Source 4">[4]</a></sup> For planning real work, the checks around an agent matter as much as its best result.

Inside companies building these tools, the change is already visible in everyday engineering. Anthropic reports that Claude authored more than 80 percent of the lines merged into its production codebase as of May 2026.<sup id="cite-5-1"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Source 5">[5]</a></sup> That is a company report about code volume, not an independent measure of productivity. It does, however, describe a workflow in which producing the text of a program is increasingly delegated.

JetBrains' survey of more than 15,000 professional developers, conducted between May and July 2026, reports 90 percent using coding agents at least weekly and 68 percent daily.<sup id="cite-6-1"><a href="#source-6" aria-label="Source 6">[6]</a></sup> Adoption tells us that a tool is becoming normal. It does not tell us how much useful work it adds, or who benefits.

> The job changed before the title did.

I would be cautious about turning any of this into a date for the disappearance of a profession. Capability, adoption, productivity, and employment are different things. They affect one another, but they do not move together. The evidence becomes more useful when we keep those distinctions in view.

<h2 id="the-job-market">The job market moved first at the bottom of the ladder</h2>

The labor-market evidence is uneven. A Danish study using administrative records through June 2024 found no significant effects of chatbot adoption on earnings or recorded hours.<sup id="cite-7-1"><a href="#source-7" aria-label="Source 7">[7]</a></sup> Stanford's US payroll analysis finds no widespread, economy-wide displacement associated with AI either.<sup id="cite-8-1"><a href="#source-8" aria-label="Source 8">[8]</a></sup> It does find a troubling pattern at the start of people's careers.

In Stanford's August 2026 update, employment among 22-to-25-year-olds in highly AI-exposed occupations was, as of June 2026, about 19 percent below where it would have been had it kept pace with similarly aged workers in less-exposed occupations. On the same measure, the gap was 15 percent in the July 2025 data. The adjustment appears to come mainly through reduced hiring, and experienced workers show no comparable gap. The authors explicitly describe these as patterns, not causal estimates: the data cannot establish how much of the divergence AI caused.<sup id="cite-8-2"><a href="#source-8" aria-label="Source 8">[8]</a></sup>

That finding can coexist with the US Bureau of Labor Statistics projecting 10 percent growth in software developer employment between 2025 and 2035.<sup id="cite-9-1"><a href="#source-9" aria-label="Source 9">[9]</a></sup> One concerns recent differences between groups of young workers; the other concerns total employment over a decade. Neither settles what an individual graduate should expect. Together they raise a practical question: if employers still need experienced people, how will the next generation get that experience?

Germany, where I work, adds another complication. Bitkom estimates 79,000 unfilled IT positions in September 2026, down from 149,000 in 2023. Among surveyed companies that have cut positions or expect to do so, 35 percent say AI replaces vacant IT roles they cannot or no longer want to fill.<sup id="cite-10-1"><a href="#source-10" aria-label="Source 10">[10]</a></sup> That is evidence of changing staffing decisions, but it does not prove that AI caused the entire decline in vacancies. A shortage of particular skills can coexist with difficult hiring conditions for applicants.

My concern is the training problem behind those numbers. Routine implementation, small fixes, and first attempts at testing have also been ways to learn a system. If agents take on more of that work, teams will need to be deliberate about how newcomers learn to judge the results. Asking for experienced judgment while removing opportunities to develop it is a poor succession plan.

<h2 id="five-responsibilities">What the work is turning into</h2>

In my own work, I find it useful to separate five responsibilities: the **builder**, the **maintainer**, the **scanner**, the **idea-shaper**, and the **operator**. These are familiar parts of IT work, not five new professions. My argument is that delegating implementation changes their balance—and makes the boundaries between job titles less useful.

Here is how I map today's jobs onto them. Multiple destinations are intentional. A title cannot tell you where someone's strongest contribution will be.

<h2 id="the-builder">01 / The builder</h2>

**Where developers, integration engineers, and automation specialists are heading.**

The builder takes a rough requirement through to a working, integrated service. With agents handling more implementation, one person may be able to cover a larger slice of that process. In February 2026, OpenAI described an experiment that produced roughly a million lines across application code, infrastructure, tooling, and documentation, with 1,500 merged pull requests in five months, initially driven by three engineers, with the team later growing to seven. The code was agent-written; the engineers concentrated on the environment, instructions, and feedback that made the work possible.<sup id="cite-11-1"><a href="#source-11" aria-label="Source 11">[11]</a></sup> It is one team's account under unusual conditions, but the shift in effort is recognizable.

That matches my week. I write the specification, the acceptance checks, and the constraints: which data may leave the server, which library we already use, what the person in the school office actually sees. The agent writes the implementation. I read it. The skill that gained value is knowing what "done" means for a real organization. The skill that lost value is remembering syntax.

The useful question for a builder is how much of the outcome they can understand and verify. A working screen is a start. It still needs to fit the organization's data, permissions, existing systems, and actual habits. Faster implementation gives those decisions more room—or makes it easier to rush past them.

<h2 id="the-maintainer">02 / The maintainer</h2>

**Where legacy developers, database administrators, ERP specialists, and experienced sysadmins are heading.**

Software can outlive the people and agents that wrote it. Someone has to keep it understandable. That is the maintainer, and AI creates both extra work and new ways to handle it.

The difficult part is that plausible code can accumulate faster than anyone understands it. Veracode's spring 2026 update reports security pass rates around 55 percent across its generation tests, with some reasoning models reaching 70–72 percent.<sup id="cite-12-1"><a href="#source-12" aria-label="Source 12">[12]</a></sup> Those are results on a particular security test, not the proportion of all AI code that is safe. They are still a reason to resist treating a passing demo as evidence that a system is ready to maintain.

The same tools can help with migrations and dependency updates. Google's experience report on internal code migrations describes substantial use of AI-generated edits alongside human review.<sup id="cite-13-1"><a href="#source-13" aria-label="Source 13">[13]</a></sup> For an organization with a WordPress shop, a Moodle server, and a Django app, help with that work is valuable. The benefit depends on knowing what must continue to work after the change.

The maintainer's advantage is knowing what depends on what. The maintainer's new job is turning that knowledge into something agents can act on safely: dependency maps, regression checks, and written rules about what must never change.

<h2 id="the-scanner">03 / The scanner</h2>

**Where testers, security analysts, and anyone who investigates technology are heading.**

A scanner investigates a system, a claim, or a proposal and produces evidence a decision can rest on. The name covers work already done by testers, security analysts, and researchers. As generated output grows, I expect this responsibility to become more prominent.

Sonar's January 2026 survey reports a revealing gap: 96 percent of surveyed developers did not fully trust AI-generated code to be functionally correct, but only 48 percent said they always checked it before committing.<sup id="cite-14-1"><a href="#source-14" aria-label="Source 14">[14]</a></sup> This is a vendor survey of reported behavior, not an audit of repositories. The tension it describes is familiar: accepting an answer takes less effort than establishing that it is right.

Verification has its own costs. Generating more changes does not automatically create more capacity to review them, even when agents help with testing. The test engineer who evaluates a service whose answers vary from run to run, the security analyst who checks an agent's permissions and actions, and the researcher who tests a demo's claims all help decide what deserves trust.

The standard I want to hold my own work to is deliberate verification. Changes involving authentication, payments, or personal data deserve close review and tests of the failure cases. Other changes still need checks proportionate to what could go wrong. Reading a diff is part of building the software; so is finding out whether it behaves as expected.

<h2 id="the-idea-shaper">04 / The idea-shaper</h2>

**Where product managers, business analysts, UX researchers, and domain experts are heading.**

When a working prototype becomes easier to make, more people can participate in shaping it. A domain expert can show a workflow and discover its gaps by trying it. That expands the conversation beyond the people who know the framework.

For the idea-shaper, that is an opportunity to test assumptions sooner. The hard questions remain: what should exist, what must not happen, who owns it after launch, and whether the real problem is a missing process rather than a missing tool. A prototype can help answer those questions. It cannot appoint the owner. That distance between a promising demonstration and something people can depend on is where I expect more of the work to sit.

<h2 id="the-operator">05 / The operator</h2>

**Where IT support, cloud operations, DevOps, SRE, and MLOps professionals are heading.**

The operator keeps live services useful and answers for them when they fail. In 2026 the services include agents, and the agents are starting to run the services.

The temptation is to hand over both routine operations and the judgment around them. But investigating an alert, recommending a fix, applying it, and deciding whether the service has recovered are different levels of authority. Each needs a clear boundary. An agent that can explain a log entry should not acquire production access simply because the next step is convenient.

I run the servers. Agents can help with patches, log analysis, and runbooks. If a teacher cannot log in on a training morning, somebody still needs to decide what to restore, what to stop, and whom to inform. Somebody must be able to interrupt the automation. That authority needs to be assigned before the incident.

<h2 id="what-i-am-doing">What I am doing about it</h2>

I do not know how far this goes. Even the effect on productivity depends on the setting. METR's randomized study found 16 experienced open-source developers working in familiar repositories took 19 percent longer with early-2025 AI tools, despite believing the tools helped.<sup id="cite-15-1"><a href="#source-15" aria-label="Source 15">[15]</a></sup> Its February 2026 follow-up could not give a reliable estimate of the newer tools' effect because of selection effects and unreliable time tracking.<sup id="cite-16-1"><a href="#source-16" aria-label="Source 16">[16]</a></sup> That is a reason to measure results in the work at hand, not to assume either a universal speedup or a permanent slowdown.

For my own work, I want to keep six habits at the center:

- **Define what done means.** Write down the outcome and acceptance criteria before asking an agent to implement it.
- **Review according to consequence.** Give sensitive changes close attention and test the ways a failure would hurt the people using the system.
- **Improve the working environment.** Clear repository instructions, useful checks, and documented constraints help across many tasks.
- **Stay able to operate the result.** Know where the backups are, how to restore them, and how to stop an automation.
- **Spend time with the people who need the work.** A conversation before building can prevent a tool nobody needed.
- **Make room for people to learn.** If judgment matters more, teams need to give newcomers practice, feedback, and increasing responsibility.

For readers in one of the jobs in the table, I would start with the part of the work you understand unusually well: why an old system behaves as it does, where a test strategy has blind spots, what a department really needs, or how to bring a failing service back. Then ask which adjacent skills would let you take responsibility for more of the outcome. These tools change what we can delegate. We still have to decide what we are prepared to stand behind.

Nineteen months ago I typed most of my code. Today I read it, question it, and decide whether it ships. The job title has stayed the same. The work inside it has changed.
